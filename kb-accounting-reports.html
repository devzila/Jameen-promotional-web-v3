How to read the accounting reports

What each accounting report answers, what it contains, and how the dates change the figures — from the trial balance and balance sheet to aging lists and the health check.

This guide is for the managing committee, the treasurer, and the society’s accountant. It explains each report in the accounting section: what question it answers, what it contains, and how the dates change the figures.

The reports are the books. They are built from posted journals. A journal that has been marked reversed is left out. Nothing in these reports is typed in by hand. A bill, a receipt, a waiver, a late fee, or a vendor bill appears here only after it has been posted.

How those journals are created is described in How Jameen keeps the society’s accounts. The society’s financial year is the Indian year: 1 April to 31 March.

A company administrator sees every society. Any other user sees the societies assigned to them. Choosing one society limits the report to that society. Leaving the society blank adds the societies that person is allowed to see.

These reports are the society’s management accounts. They are not a GST return, and they do not replace the chartered accountant.

What you can narrow

Most reports accept the same limits. The health check does not: it always covers the whole company.

LimitWhat it does
SocietyOne society, or every society you are allowed to see
From and ToThe period. On a statement of position, To is the “as at” date. If To is left blank, the report uses today
AccountOne account from the chart of accounts. Required for the account statement. Optional on the trial balance, the income report, the expense report, and the payables list
FlatJournals, or open bills, that belong to that flat
PartyA vendor, or another named party, on the journal line or the document
SearchOn a ledger, the journal number, the reference, or the description. On receivables aging, the bill number. On payables, the expense number

On a long ledger or a long list of bills, the lines are shown a page at a time. The totals at the top cover the whole selection, not only the page on the screen.

If From is left blank, there is no opening balance to carry in. The period starts from the beginning of the books and runs up to To.

Trial Balance

Purpose. The trial balance is the first check that the books are complete. Every account with a balance or a movement is listed, and the closing debits must equal the closing credits.

Use it at month end and at 31 March, before you rely on the income and expenditure account or the balance sheet. If it does not balance, stop and open the health check. Do not close the year on an unbalanced trial balance.

Each account shows:

  • opening debit and opening credit, which is the balance before From
  • debit and credit during the period, from From through To
  • closing debit and closing credit, which is the balance at To

An account with no opening, no movement, and no closing balance is left off. A total row adds the columns. The report says whether the closing debits equal the closing credits.

For the financial year, set From to 1 April and To to 31 March. The opening column is then the position at 31 March of the previous year. The movement columns are the year. The closing column is the position at this 31 March.

Debit and credit are the two sides of the books. Cash, bank, and money members still owe normally sit on the debit side. Income, vendor bills still to pay, and members’ unallocated money normally sit on the credit side. An account that has swung the other way shows its balance on the opposite side.

Income & Expenditure

Purpose. This is the society’s result for a period: income earned, expenditure recorded, and the surplus or deficit.

Use it for the monthly committee note and for the annual accounts. It answers “how did we do between these two dates?” It does not say what cash is in the bank. Cash is on the cash and bank report and on the balance sheet.

The report has three parts.

  • Income. Each income account that had a net movement in the period, including Maintenance Income and Late Fee Income. The amount is the net credit. GST charged on a bill is inside that income figure. There is no separate GST line. See the accounting guide.
  • Expenditure. Each expense account that had a net movement in the period.
  • Surplus / (Deficit). Income minus expenditure. A positive figure is a surplus. A negative figure is a deficit.

Set From and To to the period you want. For the year, use 1 April and 31 March. An account with no net movement in that period is left off, so a quiet income head does not appear as a zero row.

This report follows the journal date. A bill dated in March is this year’s income even if the resident pays in April. The April receipt does not become April income.

Balance Sheet

Purpose. The balance sheet is the position on one date: what the society holds, what it owes, and the society’s funds.

Use it at month end and at 31 March. It answers “where do we stand on this date?” The From date does not change these figures. Only To does. If To is blank, the date is today.

The report has four parts.

  • Assets. Cash in Hand, Bank Accounts, Resident Receivables, Other Income Receivables, and any other asset account with a balance. A debit balance is money the society holds or is owed.
  • Liabilities. Accounts Payable, Unallocated Receipts, Resident Advances, security deposits, and any other liability with a balance. Unallocated Receipts is money members have paid above a bill and the office has not yet applied. Resident Advances should be zero once a waiver has been applied to its bill. Both are explained in the accounting guide.
  • Equity / Society funds. The society’s fund accounts, plus a line called Current surplus / (deficit).
  • Liabilities + equity. This total is compared with total assets. The difference in the summary should be zero. A difference means the books do not agree with themselves. Open the health check.

The current surplus on this report is income minus expenditure from the start of the books up to To. It is the accumulated result, not only the months between From and To. The year’s own surplus is the figure on Income & Expenditure.

A member’s unpaid bill increases Resident Receivables. Paying it moves that balance to Cash in Hand or Bank Accounts. The income was already recognised when the bill was raised, so paying it does not increase the surplus again.

Income Report

Purpose. A list of the income accounts for the period, with the debit, the credit, and the net amount of each.

Use it when the committee wants the makeup of income: maintenance, late fees, advertisement, and any other income head. Income & Expenditure shows the same income on the way to the surplus. This report is the income on its own, with both sides of each account visible.

The net is credits minus debits. A waiver that reverses income appears as a debit and reduces the net. The total net should match Total income on Income & Expenditure for the same society and the same dates.

GST sits inside the income account, as on the income and expenditure account.

Expense Report

Purpose. A list of the expense accounts for the period, with the debit, the credit, and the net amount of each.

Use it to see where the money went: electricity, repairs, and the other heads the society has opened. The net is debits minus credits. A reversal of an expense reduces the net.

The total net should match Total expenditure on Income & Expenditure for the same society and the same dates.

An expense appears here when it is posted, which is when the vendor bill is approved. Paying the vendor later does not appear on this report. That payment appears in the cash book or the bank book, and it reduces Accounts Payable.

Cash / Bank Report

Purpose. A short statement of the two money accounts: Cash in Hand, and Bank Accounts. For each it shows the opening balance, receipts in the period, payments in the period, and the closing balance.

Use it for the treasurer’s cash position. It answers “what did we start with, what came in, what went out, and what is left?” The line-by-line detail is the cash book and the bank book.

Receipts are money in. Payments are money out. The closing balance is opening, plus receipts, minus payments.

Cash in Hand is office cash. Bank Accounts is every receipt and payment that is not cash: cheque, UPI, NEFT, RTGS, and IMPS. They share one bank account in the standard chart. If the society has added a further bank account of its own, that account is on the trial balance and can be opened as an account statement. It is not a third row on this report.

For a month, set From to the 1st and To to the last day. The opening balance should equal the previous month’s closing balance. The closing cash balance should be counted against the cash in the office. The closing bank balance should be agreed to the bank statement. Items on the bank statement that the office has not yet recorded will be the difference.

General Ledger

Purpose. Every posted journal line in the period, for every account, in date order.

Use it when you do not yet know which account a figure sits on, or when an auditor wants the full trail for a period. Each line shows the date, the journal number, the description, the account, the debit, the credit, the society, the flat or the party where there is one, and the bill, receipt, expense, or note the journal came from.

Until you pick one account, there is no running balance. Opening and closing in the summary are blank, because a running balance across mixed accounts would not mean anything. The summary still shows the total debit and the total credit of the lines in the period. Those two totals should match.

Pick one account and the general ledger becomes that account’s statement: an opening balance, a running balance after each line, and a closing balance.

Search finds a journal number, a reference, or words in the description.

Account Statement

Purpose. One account, line by line, with a running balance. You choose the account before the report will run.

Use it for any account that does not have its own named book. Other Income Receivables, Late Fee Income, Unallocated Receipts, a particular expense head, and a bank account the society added itself are all read here.

The summary shows:

  • opening balance, which is the account’s balance before From
  • total debit and total credit in the period
  • closing balance at To

The balance column walks from the opening balance through each line to the closing balance. On an asset account such as cash or a receivable, receipts and new bills increase the balance when they are debits. On an income account, bills increase the balance when they are credits.

The flat limit keeps lines that came from that flat’s bills, receipts, credit notes, or late fees. A vendor payment has no flat, so it stays in the statement only when you are not limiting by flat.

Resident Receivable Ledger

Purpose. The ledger of Resident Receivables: what members were billed, what they paid, what was waived, and what is still owed, as movements on that one account.

Use it to answer “how did the members’ outstanding balance get from last month’s figure to this month’s?” and to trace one flat. Filter by flat for a single allotment. The running balance is the members’ dues in the books.

A new bill debits this account. A receipt, a waiver applied to a bill, and a credit note applied to a bill credit it. A late fee debits it again. The closing balance should agree with the Resident Receivables line on the trial balance and the balance sheet for the same To date.

This ledger is members only. Money owed by an advertiser or another non-member sits on Other Income Receivables. Open that account with an account statement. Both kinds of open bill appear together on Receivables Aging.

Vendor Payable Ledger

Purpose. The ledger of Accounts Payable: vendor bills the society has approved, and the payments made against them.

Use it to see how the amount owed to vendors moved in the period. Approving an expense credits this account. Paying it debits this account. The closing balance is what the books say is still owed to vendors.

Filter by party to see one vendor. The list of individual unpaid bills, with how late each one is, is the Payables report. This ledger is the account. That report is the documents.

The closing balance should agree with Accounts Payable on the trial balance and the balance sheet.

Cash Book

Purpose. Cash in Hand, line by line. Every posted movement of office cash in the period, with a running balance.

Use it to check the cash box. The closing balance is the cash the books say is on hand at To. Count the cash on that date. The two figures should match. A difference is cash not yet recorded, or a receipt recorded in the wrong place.

Money received in cash increases the balance. Cash paid out, including a vendor paid in cash, decreases it. A cheque or a transfer does not appear here. Those are in the bank book.

The opening balance is the cash balance before From. For a daily check, set From and To to the same day. The lines are that day’s cash, and the closing balance is the cash at the end of that day.

Bank Book

Purpose. Bank Accounts, line by line. Every posted movement that the office recorded as cheque, UPI, NEFT, RTGS, or IMPS, with a running balance.

Use it to agree the books to the bank. Work from the closing balance at To back to the bank statement on that date. Receipts not yet on the statement, and payments on the statement not yet in the books, are the reconciling items.

The standard chart keeps one Bank Accounts ledger for all of those instruments. A second bank account, if the society has created one, is read as an account statement, not in this book.

The opening balance is the bank balance in the books before From.

Receivables Aging

Purpose. Every bill that still has something unpaid, grouped by how late it is. This is the collection list.

Use it for the arrears meeting: who to remind, who is slightly late, and who has been outstanding for more than three months. The summary is the total still unpaid and the number of bills. The buckets are:

BucketThe due date, compared with To (or today)
CurrentDue today or later. The bill is not yet overdue
1–30d1 to 30 days past the due date
31–60d31 to 60 days past the due date
61–90d61 to 90 days past the due date
90+dMore than 90 days past the due date

Each row is one bill: its number, the bill date, the due date, the amount still outstanding, the bucket, the number of days overdue, the society, the income account, and the flat or the party.

The amount still outstanding is the unpaid bill after receipts and waivers, plus any late fee on that bill that is still open. A bill that is fully settled, including its late fees, is left off. A cancelled bill is left off. A partly paid bill stays on the list for the remainder.

Member bills and non-member bills are both on this list. Filter by flat for one allotment, or by party for one outside party. Search by bill number.

The rupee amounts are what is unpaid now. To changes the age, not the rupees. A bill due on 1 March is in 1–30d if To is 20 March, and in 31–60d if To is 20 April, provided it is still unpaid when you open the report. From is not used to drop bills.

The total outstanding should be read with Resident Receivables and Other Income Receivables on the balance sheet. Those ledger balances are the posted journals. This list is the open documents. They meet when every open bill and late fee has been posted and nothing else is sitting on those receivable accounts.

Payables

Purpose. Every approved vendor bill that is not fully paid, grouped by how late it is. This is the payment list.

Use it to decide what the society should pay next. The buckets are the same as receivables aging: Current, 1–30 days, 31–60, 61–90, and more than 90 days. The age runs from the expense due date. If the expense has no due date, the age runs from the expense date.

Each row shows the expense number, the expense date, the due date, the bill amount, the amount still unpaid, the bucket, the days overdue, the vendor, the expense account, and the description.

Only approved expenses appear. A reversed expense is left off. A fully paid expense is left off. Filter by vendor, or by expense account, to see one supplier or one kind of cost. Search by expense number.

The total still unpaid should agree with the Accounts Payable balance when every approved unpaid expense has been posted and no other balance is sitting on that account.

Paying an expense does not remove it from the year’s expenditure. The expense report keeps the cost. This report only drops the bill once the payment covers it.

Accounting Health Check

Purpose. A pass over the whole company that asks whether the books are internally consistent. It is the report to open when the trial balance does not balance, when a balance-sheet difference is not zero, or before the year is closed and handed to the auditor.

Use it as a yes-or-no list. Each line is a check. A tick means that check found nothing. A cross means it found problems, and the number beside it is how many. The check covers every society of the company. It does not take a date range or a single society.

CheckWhat a tick means
All journals balancedEvery posted or reversed journal has debits equal to credits
Trial balance balancedAdding every posted line, the debits equal the credits
No orphan journal linesEvery journal has lines. None is an empty shell
No journal references inactive accountsNo line points at an account that has been switched off
No journals in closed periodsNothing was posted into a financial year after that year was closed
Invoice → journal reconciliation passedEvery member bill that is not cancelled has one posted journal for the bill total, and a cancelled bill is not still standing as an ordinary posted journal
Payment → journal reconciliation passedThe same test for completed receipts, and for receipts that were reversed
Expense → journal reconciliation passedThe same test for approved vendor bills, and for vendor bills that were reversed
External income → journal reconciliation passedThe same test for non-member bills
No duplicate journal postingsThe same document was not posted twice
No orphan accounting recordsEvery journal points at a bill, receipt, expense, debit note, or credit note that still exists, and every reversal points at the journal it reverses

A cross is for the accountant to clear before the figures are circulated. The health check does not change the books. It only reports.

A reading order at the year end

On 31 March, or on the day the committee looks at the year:

  1. Open the health check. Clear any cross before circulating figures.
  2. Open the trial balance from 1 April to 31 March. Confirm that it balances, and that the closing column is the position you expect to carry forward.
  3. Open Income & Expenditure for the same dates. That surplus or deficit is the year’s result. The income report and the expense report are the detail behind it.
  4. Open the balance sheet as at 31 March. Assets should equal liabilities plus equity. The current surplus includes this year’s result and the results of earlier years.
  5. Open the cash and bank report for the year. Agree cash to the count and bank to the statement.
  6. Open receivables aging and payables as at 31 March. These are the arrears and the unpaid vendors that sit inside the balance sheet.

For a single flat, open the resident receivable ledger filtered to that flat, then receivables aging filtered to the same flat. The ledger is the history. The aging list is what is still open.

What the office should remember

  • Posted journals make the figures. A reversed journal is left out. The reports are not a second set of books.
  • Income & Expenditure is the period between From and To. The balance sheet is the position at To. The year’s surplus and the accumulated surplus are different numbers.
  • Income includes GST. Paying a bill is not new income. Paying a vendor is not a new expense.
  • Cash in Hand and Bank Accounts are the two standard money accounts. The cash book, the bank book, and the cash and bank report all read those accounts.
  • Resident Receivables is members. Other Income Receivables is everyone else. Receivables aging lists both, bill by bill, including open late fees.
  • The vendor payable ledger is the account. The payables report is the unpaid vendor bills, with their age.
  • The totals on a paged report cover the whole selection. The health check covers the whole company, and it does not post or correct anything itself.
